Healthcare & communication

A message someone can use

A draft on making public information approachable, specific and honest about its limits.

This essay was newly drafted for local review. It is not presented as a previously published work. Any constructed example is identified in the text.

Start with the encounter

A piece of health information can be accurate and still be difficult to enter. A reader might meet it while passing a stand, glancing at a small printed item or watching a demonstration. Those are different situations. Treating them as identical can produce a message that contains plenty of detail but gives the person encountering it no clear place to begin.

The Orbis materials on this site make that question tangible. They include a campus booth, printed education materials and an interactive demonstration. My documented contribution was community outreach planning as a Group Leader. The photographs show the formats and setting; they do not tell us what every visitor understood. That boundary is a useful starting point for this draft: communication should be examined through both the material and the evidence available about its use.

Decide what the reader needs first

One practical approach is to write the reader’s question before writing the heading. What is this display about? Why might I spend a moment with it? Where can I ask for more information? These are modest questions, but they can make the difference between a collection of statements and an intelligible invitation to learn.

The CDC Clear Communication Index asks communicators to identify an audience, objective and main message, then keep the main message prominent and describe an appropriate action. That is useful guidance for the design question here, rather than evidence about the performance of a particular event. CDC: how to use the Index.

Give the formats different jobs

Imagine a hypothetical campus display with three elements: a large heading, a short information card and a demonstration. This is an editorial exercise, not a reconstruction of an unrecorded Orbis interaction. The heading could establish the topic. The card could organise a small set of points. The demonstration could create an opening for a volunteer to explain what the activity represents.

Copying the same dense paragraph onto all three would ignore their differences. A heading is read at a distance; a card can be held and revisited; an activity unfolds over time. A stronger arrangement would let each contribute a different piece of understanding while keeping the relationship between them recognisable. The issue is the distribution of information, rather than a decorative preference for fewer words.

Preserve the distinction between an illustration and a conclusion

Visual material can give a subject immediate presence. It can also be asked to carry too much meaning. A demonstration may illustrate an idea without measuring a person’s condition. A campaign photograph can document a setting without proving a change in knowledge or behaviour. The words around the material should help a reader understand what kind of evidence they are seeing.

This matters for the portfolio as much as for a public display. A case study gains credibility when it says what was done, shows what can be shown and stops before an unsupported result. Leaving that boundary visible can be more useful than supplying a confident conclusion simply because the page seems to need one.

A next step should belong to the message

A next step is strongest when it follows from the purpose of the material. For an introductory display, that might be a route to a fuller explanation or a place to ask a question. It should not become a dramatic instruction added merely to make the design feel decisive. WHO’s communication framework similarly considers awareness, understanding and action as related stages, rather than assuming one exposure is sufficient. WHO: moving audiences toward action.

In the hypothetical display, the useful test would be whether someone can identify the topic, explain the main point in their own terms and find the intended route onward. Those would be proposed checks for a future communication exercise. They are not outcomes already measured in the project recorded here.

Leave room to listen

My provisional view is that clarity begins before the final sentence is polished. It begins with choosing an audience and deciding which question the material is trying to answer. Editing can then make the structure visible: a main point, supporting detail and a proportionate next step.

The question I would keep open is how the material changes after listening to its audience. What was immediately understood, what needed explanation and what was misunderstood? Those observations would make a future revision more grounded. Until they exist, the honest task is to describe the work carefully and offer a reasoned design proposal without treating that proposal as a proven effect.
