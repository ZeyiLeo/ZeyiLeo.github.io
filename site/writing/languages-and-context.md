## Fluency does not settle every question

A sentence can be grammatically clear and still leave a listener outside the conversation. The listener may not know the work being discussed, the reference a speaker assumes or the reason a particular image carries weight. In public expression, the language of delivery and the context of understanding are connected tasks, but they are not the same task.

My interests in literature, languages, cinema and traditional theatre provide a background for considering that distinction. The April 2025 China–Latin American Literature Roundtable provides a documented public occasion: a keynote and participation in discussion. This essay develops a question prompted by that kind of setting. It does not reproduce the speech, quote another participant or claim that a particular interpretation was agreed at the event.

## An explanation is also a choice

Consider a hypothetical introduction to an unfamiliar play. The speaker could begin with the title and a plot summary, explain a convention of performance, or describe why one scene deserves attention. Each is a different invitation to the audience. Giving every possible piece of background would not necessarily make the introduction more useful; the details need a relationship to the argument being made.

This example is a constructed exercise, not a memory of an actual presentation. Its point is that context has to be selected. The choice is worth making deliberately because it shapes what a listener is able to notice next. An explanation can open a work without pretending to supply the only possible route into it.

## Keep a particular thing particular

One temptation in cross-cultural discussion is to replace an unfamiliar reference with a familiar one and move on. A comparison can help, but it can also remove the detail that made the original example worth discussing. The audience may understand the analogy while losing sight of the work itself.

A different approach would be to give one specific detail enough room. In the hypothetical play introduction, that might mean describing the relationship between a spoken line and its performed gesture before offering a comparison. The explanation stays anchored in the example. The speaker can then say where a comparison helps and where it stops, rather than letting the comparison become a substitute for the subject.

## An address and a conversation unfold differently

A prepared address can develop a line of thought in a chosen sequence. A roundtable creates another situation: other contributions may shift what needs explanation, and a response may need to be shorter or framed differently. My CV records participation in both forms at the literature event. The distinction itself is a useful subject for reflection, even without an available transcript of the discussion.

For a speaker, a possible discipline is to separate the point that must remain clear from the wording used to carry it. A listener's question might reveal that the missing piece is not another argument, but a reference that was never explained. Responding to that gap can serve the conversation more effectively than repeating a prepared passage unchanged.

## Images can offer context, with limits

The photographs in the visual journal show an occasion and a speaking setting. They can help a visitor locate a person and a public activity, but they cannot preserve the whole argument. The written account has to do a different job: identify the role, subject and documented contribution without inventing a response from the room.

Film and visual storytelling raise a related question about sequence. An image gains meaning through what comes before and after it. For this draft, that is a way to think about explanation: the order in which a reference is introduced can change how much work the next sentence asks of an audience. It is an editorial analogy, not a claim of formal film scholarship.

## A provisional standard for the conversation

The standard I would propose is modest: make it possible for someone new to the reference to follow the point, while leaving enough detail for the original subject to retain its character. That requires both accessibility and restraint. An explanation should support attention, rather than insisting that every uncertainty has been removed.

The question for further reading is what an audience needs to remain curious. Sometimes that may be a fuller context; sometimes one well-chosen example is enough to begin. A useful discussion can acknowledge that people arrive with different references and still give them something particular to consider together.
