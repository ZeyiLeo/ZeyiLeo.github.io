The work

Public-health communication begins with how people encounter an idea. My Orbis work connects public-health and international charity projects with community outreach planning.

As Group Leader, I worked with cross-functional teams to develop outreach plans. The campaign documentation includes a glaucoma-awareness booth, printed education materials, and an interactive demonstration.

A closer look

The booth brings the subject into an everyday university setting. Posters, materials, and conversations create several ways for people to engage with eye-health information.

What connects

This project sits between healthcare and communication: understanding the topic, considering the audience, and helping a team turn information into a public encounter.

Three ways into one subject

The project materials show three different kinds of encounter. A booth gives the campaign a physical presence on campus. Printed pieces carry information in a form someone can inspect at their own pace. The interactive demonstration introduces an activity. Looking at these together is more informative than selecting one photograph as a complete account of the campaign.

The booth photograph also contains context: university buildings, campaign displays and the working surface of a public stand. These details situate the project in an everyday campus environment. They do not establish how long each visitor stayed or what anyone learned. The distinction matters when a portfolio uses an attractive image to explain work that was partly organisational and partly communicative.

Planning an encounter, not only a poster

My recorded contribution is community outreach planning with cross-functional teams, in a Group Leader role. That places the emphasis on bringing different parts of a public activity together. The visible materials are useful evidence of the setting and formats, while the role describes the contribution I made around them.

An editorial way to examine the project is to ask what each format is best placed to do. A display can establish the subject and draw attention. A small printed item can organise a limited amount of information. A demonstration can provide an opening for explanation.

A communication question worth carrying forward

The central question is how to make an unfamiliar health topic approachable without letting visual impact carry more certainty than the content supports. That question connects this case with my draft essay on usable health information. The essay develops a hypothetical information-design exercise rather than inventing conversations with visitors or reporting clinical outcomes.

A next evaluation could separate three questions: whether a message was noticed, whether its main point was understood, and whether an appropriate next step was clear. Those would require different forms of evidence. A photograph can help answer where and how the campaign was presented; it cannot answer all three questions on its own. This provides a useful direction for thinking about future documentation without rewriting the results of the existing project.

Continue through the material

The process story above moves from the campus campaign to the printed pieces and the demonstration. The visual journal lets visitors revisit those photographs with their context kept beside them. For a different view of health-sciences communication, the U21 case follows the move from research and group discussion to recommendations for an academic audience.
